Cree Syllabics · ~160 syllables · James Evans · 1840

Geometry as Language

In 1840, Methodist missionary James Evans created a writing system for the Cree language that used a brilliant geometric principle: the same base shape, rotated to indicate different vowels.

Point the triangle right → e. Point it up → i. Point it left → o. Point it down → a. This rotation principle means that once you learn one consonant series, you already know how every other consonant behaves.

The system spread rapidly across Canada. It was adapted for Ojibwe, Inuktitut, Blackfoot, Athabaskan languages, and more. Today it's used officially in Nunavut and across First Nations communities. The syllabics appear on Canadian currency and public signage throughout the north.

A final consonant (with no following vowel) is written as a small superscript form of the character — called a final. For example, the standalone "p" final is written as a small ᑉ.

Why It Works So Well

Evans' genius was making the system visually systematic. Unlike Cherokee or Bamum (where each syllable has an arbitrary shape), Canadian Syllabics encodes information in the orientation of each glyph. A learner who understands the rotation principle can read unfamiliar consonant series immediately. Literacy rates among Cree speakers surged after adoption — reportedly higher than literacy rates among English-speaking Canadians at the time.
