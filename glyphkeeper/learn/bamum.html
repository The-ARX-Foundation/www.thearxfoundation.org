A Ka U Ku — Learn the Bamum Syllabary

80 characters · King Ibrahim Njoya · Foumban, Cameroon · c. 1918

Born from a Dream

In 1896, the illiterate King Ibrahim Njoya of the Bamum Kingdom in western Cameroon had a revelatory dream. A teacher instructed him to draw an image on a wooden tablet, wash it off, and drink the water — and through this ritual, receive knowledge. Upon waking, Njoya re-enacted the dream and began work on a writing system for his people.

What followed was one of the most remarkable feats of writing system evolution ever documented: over fourteen years, Njoya and his advisors iterated the script through seven major revisions, compressing 465 pictograms down to 80 syllabic characters.

The Seven Phases

1896
Lewa ("Book") — The first draft. 465 pictograms Multi-directional writing. Pure pictographic proto-writing.
1899
Mbima ("Mixed") — Simplified: 72 cut, 45 added. 437 chars Writing direction fixed to left-to-right.
1902
Nyi Nyi Nfa' — Further reduction. 371 chars Used to write the History of the Bamun People.
1907
Rii Nyi Nsha Mfw' — Continued simplification. 285 chars
~1908
Rii Nyi Mfw' Men — Used for Bible translation. 195 chars Last pictographic stage.
1910
A Ka U Ku — The breakthrough: a full syllabary. 82 chars + 10 digits Could represent 160 distinct syllables. Used for births, marriages, deaths, and court rulings.
1918
Mfemfe ("New") — The final refinement. 80 characters Copper type cast for printing. Njoya wrote histories, a pharmacopoeia, maps, legal codes, and a calendar in this script.

Suppression & Revival

When the French took control of western Cameroon after WWI, they saw Njoya's literate kingdom as a threat. They smashed his printing press, burned his libraries, destroyed his books, and exiled him to Yaoundé, where he died in 1933. The script fell into disuse.

But the script survived. Njoya's son and grandson preserved manuscripts and held the writing as a cultural symbol. In 2007, the Bamum Scripts and Archives Project began reviving the script — teaching it to young people, digitizing threatened documents, and building computer fonts.

Today, the 80 glyphs of A Ka U Ku Mfemfe are encoded in Unicode, waiting to be learned. That's what you're here to do.
