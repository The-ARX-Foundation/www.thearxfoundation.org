~195 characters · Kisimi Kamara · Sierra Leone · c. 1921

The Script That Challenged an Empire

In colonial Sierra Leone, Kisimi Kamara (c. 1890–1962) saw that the British controlled his country partly through their literacy. He decided to give the Mende people that same power.

Building on the 42-character foundation created by Islamic scholar Mohamed Turay around 1917, Kamara expanded the system to over 195 syllabic characters and promoted it across southern Sierra Leone. The script was named Kikakui after its first three sounds: Ki, Ka, Ku.

Kikakui drew inspiration from three sources: the Vai syllabary from neighboring Liberia, the Arabic abjad (Turay was an Islamic scholar), and indigenous Mende graphic symbols. The result was unique — a right-to-left syllabary that became widely used for correspondence, record-keeping, and Quranic transcription.

By the 1930s, Kikakui was thriving. But in the 1940s, the British colonial government established the Protectorate Literacy Bureau and actively promoted the Latin alphabet, suppressing indigenous scripts. Kikakui usage declined sharply.

Today fewer than 500 people use it — but revival efforts are underway. It was encoded in Unicode 7.0 (2014), and scholars like Konrad Tuchscherer are teaching classes in Sierra Leone with intense student interest.

Note: Mende Kikakui characters are in the Unicode Supplementary Multilingual Plane (U+1E800–1E8DF) and may not render in all browsers. If you see boxes, you may need to install a Mende Kikakui font.
