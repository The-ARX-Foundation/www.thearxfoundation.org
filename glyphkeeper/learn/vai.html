ꕙꔤ — Learn the Vai Syllabary

~300 characters · Momolu Duwalu Bukele · Liberia · c. 1833

Born in Liberia, Possibly Inspired by Cherokee

Momolu Duwalu Bukele of Jondu, in what is now Grand Cape Mount County, Liberia, is credited with creating the Vai syllabary around 1832–33. It is one of the most successful indigenous scripts in West Africa.

Intriguingly, a Cherokee man named Austin Curtis had settled in Vai country around 1827 — and the inscription that first drew the world's attention to the Vai script was on his house. Whether the Cherokee syllabary directly inspired the Vai system remains debated, but the timing and connection are remarkable.

By 1899, most Vai people were reportedly literate in the script. It was standardized in the 1960s at the University of Liberia, with dots and strokes added to cover all CV syllables. Today roughly 55 high-frequency characters account for 80–90% of typical Vai writing.

The script represents consonant-vowel (CV) syllables. Long vowels, diphthongs, and nasal endings are written with two characters. Tones exist in spoken Vai but are not marked in the script — context resolves ambiguity, much like Cherokee.

This learner covers the core set of ~70 essential characters — the vowels and most common consonant series that form the backbone of Vai literacy.
