Two Scripts, One Sound System

Japanese uses two complete syllabaries — Hiragana and Katakana — each containing 46 base characters that map to the same set of sounds. They're used for different purposes but are phonetically identical.

Hiragana ひらがな

あ い う え お
Rounded, flowing strokes. Used for native Japanese words, grammar particles, and verb endings. Developed from cursive Chinese characters by women of the Heian court, c. 8th–9th century.

Katakana カタカナ

ア イ ウ エ オ
Angular, sharp strokes. Used for foreign loanwords, onomatopoeia, emphasis, and scientific terms. Developed by Buddhist monks from fragments of Chinese characters.

Both scripts descend from man'yōgana — Chinese characters used purely for their phonetic value in the 8th century Man'yōshū poetry anthology. Over centuries, scribes simplified these borrowed characters into the compact kana we use today.

The 46 base characters cover 5 vowels (a, i, u, e, o) and 40 consonant-vowel pairs, plus the standalone "n" (ん/ン). With dakuten (゛) and handakuten (゜) marks, the system extends to cover all Japanese syllables — about 107 in total.

Why Learn Kana?

Kana are the gateway to Japanese literacy. Unlike kanji (which number in the thousands), kana can be learned in a few weeks — and once you know them, you can read any Japanese text that's been annotated with furigana. Every Japanese learner starts here.
