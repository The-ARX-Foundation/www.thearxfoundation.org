The Largest Syllabary Ever Standardized

The Yi scripts are among the oldest indigenous writing systems in East Asia. The Nuosu (Northern Yi) people of southwestern China's Sichuan and Yunnan provinces have used writing for centuries — the earliest known inscription dates to 1485 CE, though the tradition is likely much older.

The original Classical Yi was a logographic system with over 90,000 regional character variants — each one representing a syllable that was also a morpheme. In 1974, the Chinese government standardized 820 symbols from the Liangshan region into a modern syllabary, creating the largest standardized syllabary in the world: 1,164 unique syllables plus one iteration mark.

The system encodes 43 consonants × 8 vowels × 3–4 tones. Not all combinations exist, but the result is still enormous. Traditionally Yi was written right-to-left in vertical columns — the standardized form writes left-to-right.

The Tone System

Yi has four tones. Three have dedicated characters; the fourth (rising) uses a diacritic arc above the mid-tone character. In Yi Pinyin romanization, tones are marked by a trailing letter:

High tone (-t)

ꀀ
Sharp, elevated pitch. Pinyin: "it"

Mid tone (∅)

ꀂ
Level, neutral pitch. Pinyin: "i" (no marker)

Low tone (-p)

ꀃ
Low, falling pitch. Pinyin: "ip"

Rising tone (-x)

ꀁ
Rising pitch with arc diacritic. Pinyin: "ix"
