Linear B — The Oldest Greek Writing

~87 syllabic signs · c. 1450 BCE · Deciphered 1952 by Michael Ventris

3,400 Years Old — And Greek

Linear B is a syllabic script used to write Mycenaean Greek — the oldest attested form of the Greek language. It was used from roughly 1450 to 1200 BCE, primarily for palace administrative records: inventories of grain, oil, livestock, and personnel.

The script was adapted from the earlier Linear A (which remains undeciphered and was likely used for the Minoan language). When Mycenaean civilization collapsed during the Late Bronze Age, Linear B disappeared entirely. Greece entered a "Dark Age" with no writing until the adoption of the Phoenician alphabet centuries later.

The Decipherment

In 1952, Michael Ventris — an English architect and amateur linguist — cracked the code, building on years of statistical groundwork by Alice Kober. His key insight: the language was an early form of Greek. This was shocking — scholars had assumed Minoan, not Greek.

Ventris built his famous grid: consonants down the rows, vowels across the columns. By finding patterns in how signs alternated in word endings (like gender inflections), he could assign phonetic values. When he tested his values against place names on Cretan tablets — ko-no-so (Knossos), a-mi-ni-so (Amnisos) — everything clicked.

Tragically, Ventris died in a car crash in 1956, just four years after his breakthrough. He was 34.

How It Works

Linear B is a CV (consonant-vowel) open syllabary — each sign represents one syllable. There are no signs for bare consonants or consonant clusters, so scribes had to use workarounds: "sperma" (seed) was written pe-ma, dropping the initial s-. The q-series represents labiovelar sounds (/kʷ/) that later disappeared from Greek.
