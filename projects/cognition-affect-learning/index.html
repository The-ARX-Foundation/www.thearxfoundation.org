§ 01Introduction — The Jigsaw of Knowledge

Our species, Homo Sapiens, is the being who thinks. But we are also the beings who learn, and the beings who experience a rich spectrum of affective emotional states.

As we learn, we acquire units of knowledge — pieces of a gigantic jigsaw puzzle — and arduously weave them into a fabric of knowledge in which all the pieces fit together and a "big picture" emerges. That big picture, hidden from view until we are nearly done, is called insight.

Core claim: Learning is not steady. We learn in fits and starts, occasionally "unlearning" misconceptions. Our learning curve doesn't gracefully ascend — it wiggles. And those wiggles carry identifiable emotional signatures: curiosity, fascination, confusion, anxiety, surprise, perplexity, frustration, chagrin, despair, hope, satisfaction, and elation.

§ 02Emotion Axes — Six Candidate Dimensions

We use the term affect to subsume the spectrum of emotional states one can experience while learning. Each axis divides into a positive valence (appetitive) half and a negative valence (aversive) half. Most activities activate emotions on more than one axis — and it's entirely possible to experience an emotional tug-of-war.

§ 03The Roller Coaster Learning Curve

Learning is non-monotonic. We sometimes acquire erroneous beliefs and misconceptions which we eventually have to discard. The curve has sections where curvature is concave (downward-turning, negative emotions) or convex (upward-turning, positive emotions).

In the Calculus, this corresponds to the sign of the Second Derivative. Curve turning downward → negative 2nd derivative → negative-valence emotions. Curve turning upward → positive 2nd derivative → positive-valence emotions.

§ 07The Bardic Arts — StoryCraft

Throughout most of human history, cultural knowledge was passed by telling stories. The classroom model lost much of that power — especially in science and math, where storycraft is almost unheard of.

A story is an anecdote drawn from the culture. The two primary characters in any drama — Protagonist and Antagonist — each have a characteristic psychological profile.

For drama to work, the Protagonist and Antagonist must have complementary psychologies — the desires of one arouse the fears of the other, and vice versa. This feedback loop propels the conflict.
§ 09Clancy's Theorem & the Vexagon

The central theorem of Drama Theory — inspired by Tom Clancy and Shakespeare's Much Ado About Nothing:

Clancy's Theorem: The (vector) Sum of All Fears = Zero. Each action by some character is an emotion-driven reaction to a preceding action, and the reaction provokes a downstream response from another character. This gives dramatic continuity.
Interactive · The Vexagon Diagram
Click a character node. Each responds to a triggering remark or gesture of another in a cyclical loop. Sum of All Fears = 0.

To run a drama to Tragedy, the characters consume their resources without resolving their conflict. To run it to Comedy, something astonishing must happen — the characters solve the mystery of their long-suffering agony and discover the system model. And so must the audience.

§ 10Insightful Learning — The Full Loop

Conventional classroom pedagogy gives us Data, Information, and Knowledge — but stops there. What's missing is Wisdom (knowledge + values) and Story (wisdom in dramatic action). And the whole thing is a loop:

Interactive · The Insightful Learning Loop
Click any node. The loop: Data → Information → Knowledge → (+ Values) → Wisdom → (story making) → Anecdote → (collection) → Data. It's a Loop!
What the classroom misses: Modern educational pedagogy concentrates on the lower part of the loop — Data → Information → Knowledge. But without folding in values (dreads and desires) to reach Wisdom, and without dramatic story to carry it, the learning process is impoverished.
