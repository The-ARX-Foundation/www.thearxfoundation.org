§ 01The Commons

A public space with infrastructure but zero commercial capture. Delivery access, seating, power, wifi, water — but no entity gets to own the attention layer. No ads. No monopoly concessions. No hostile architecture. The anti-airport.

Every public space in America has been consumed by the same pattern: a captive audience is created (by necessity, by transit, by circumstance), then that attention is sold. Airports are the purest expression — $19 croissant sandwiches, $7 vitamin water, $16 avocado toast — because you literally cannot leave. The architecture funnels you through retail. The chairs prevent sleeping. The wifi harvests data. Every surface is an ad.

§ 01.1The Problem Is Structural

This isn't a complaint about prices. It's about the fundamental architecture of public space being designed to extract rather than serve. When HMSHost holds a monopoly concession at every gate, there is no market. There is no competition. There is only captive demand and monopoly pricing. The "public" space is private infrastructure wearing a public mask.

The same pattern repeats at scale: shopping malls, university campuses, hospital complexes, transit hubs, stadiums. Wherever people must be, someone is monetizing the fact that they can't leave. The Commons asks: what if we designed it the other way?

What if a public space existed where the infrastructure served the people in it — and the business model didn't require selling their attention, their data, or their dignity?

§ 02Anti-Patterns in Public Space

Monopoly Concession Model

A single vendor (HMSHost, Aramark, Delaware North) wins exclusive rights to operate all food/retail in a captive environment. Competition is eliminated by contract. Prices inflate 200-400% above street level because exit is impossible or impractical.

Found in: Airports, stadiums, hospitals, convention centers

Hostile Architecture

Benches with armrests every 18 inches. Surfaces tilted at 15° so you can't sleep. Spikes under overhangs. Sprinkler systems on timers. The built environment is weaponized against rest, presence, and human comfort — to force movement toward commercial zones.

Found in: Transit hubs, parks, airports, public plazas

Attention Monetization Layer

Every surface becomes a billboard. Digital screens cycle ads at 15-second intervals. WiFi login pages harvest email addresses. Floor decals guide you past sponsored storefronts. The space itself becomes an advertising medium, and your presence is the product being sold.

Found in: Everywhere with foot traffic

Artificial Scarcity of Basics

Water fountains removed or hidden. Power outlets removed from seating areas. Free wifi throttled to unusable speeds. These create dependency on paid alternatives: bottled water, charging stations, premium wifi. Basic human needs become revenue streams.

Found in: Airports, malls, transit stations

The Funnel

Physical architecture forces movement through retail corridors. Security checkpoints deposit you in shopping zones. Gate changes require walking past 40 storefronts. The path between A and B is never direct — it's a consumer gauntlet designed to maximize exposure to purchase opportunities.

Found in: Airports (especially post-security), IKEA, casinos

§ 03Design Principles
§ 03.1Infrastructure as Commons

Power, water, wifi, seating, shelter — provided as infrastructure, not as products. These are the baseline. Like a public library provides books and internet without selling your browsing data, The Commons provides the physical infrastructure of comfort without monetizing it. Abundant power outlets at every seat. Unrestricted high-speed wifi with zero data collection. Clean water. Flat, comfortable seating you can actually sleep on.

§ 03.2Open Delivery, Zero Tenancy

Any vendor can deliver to The Commons. No vendor occupies it. Instead of monopoly concessions, the space operates like a public square: food trucks can pull up to designated zones. Delivery drivers have clear access points. Local restaurants can deliver. You order from your phone and it arrives — at street prices, not captive prices. The space itself takes zero cut.

This breaks the HMSHost model completely. Competition is restored because any business in the surrounding area can serve the people inside. The $19 croissant becomes a $6 croissant because five bakeries are competing on DoorDash to your exact seat.

§ 03.3Zero Advertising Surface

No ads. Anywhere. Non-negotiable. No digital screens cycling sponsored content. No branded furniture. No sponsored wifi login pages. No floor decals. No "brought to you by" anything. The visual environment belongs to the people in it. Community bulletin boards — physical, non-commercial — are the only permitted information surfaces beyond wayfinding.

§ 03.4Human-First Architecture

Designed for rest, work, connection, and presence — not throughput. Flat benches long enough to lie down on. Quiet zones with acoustic treatment. Standing desks. Floor seating areas. Nap pods. Charging at every position. Natural light. Plants. Temperature controlled for comfort, not for pushing people toward heated retail zones. The architecture says: you are welcome to stay.

The measure of a public space is how it treats someone with nothing to spend. If the design assumes you're a consumer, it's not public — it's a store with extra steps.

§ 04Implementation Paths

Municipal Partnership Model

Partner with a city to convert underutilized public buildings (closed schools, defunct malls, unused transit facilities) into Commons spaces. Funded through municipal bonds or public-private partnership structures where the "private" side is a nonprofit land trust, not a developer.

Precedent: Public libraries, community centers | Scale: City-level

Community Land Trust

Acquire land through a nonprofit CLT structure that permanently removes it from speculative markets. The land can never be sold to a developer. The Commons operates on it in perpetuity. Funded by a combination of community investment, grants, and a small optional-donation model (never required, never gatekeeping).

Precedent: Burlington Community Land Trust, Dudley Street | Scale: Neighborhood

Revenue Without Extraction

Sustainable funding through voluntary memberships (like public radio), event space rental for community gatherings, maker space tool libraries, and a tiny logistics fee on deliveries (paid by vendor, not consumer, capped at cost-recovery). The key constraint: no revenue stream that requires selling attention or creating artificial scarcity.

Model: PBS/NPR hybrid | Constraint: Zero advertising revenue

Pilot: Van Life Rest Stops

Smallest viable version: a network of rest stops for van lifers and road travelers that provide the basics — power hookups, wifi, water fill, dump stations, flat parking, community kitchen — without the Walmart parking lot compromise or the $60/night KOA gouge. Prove the model works before scaling to urban.

Connection: Wellspring (ARX amenity finder) | Scale: Regional pilot

§ 05Why This Matters

Public space is disappearing into commercial space wearing a public costume. Every airport, every mall, every transit hub, every university quad — the spaces where humans gather out of necessity — have been captured by the same extraction logic. You cannot exist in these spaces without being a consumer. Your presence is the raw material; your attention is the product; your wallet is the exit fee.

The Commons is the assertion that infrastructure can serve people without monetizing them. That a space can be abundant — power, water, wifi, comfort, rest — without requiring you to buy a $7 bottle of water to justify your presence.

§ 05.1Connection to ARX

The Commons sits at the intersection of several ARX Foundation threads: Wellspring (open-source amenity mapping for travelers), Homeless to Homed (infrastructure access as a human right), the Sanctuary concept (public knowledge monasteries), and the broader post-capitalist infrastructure vision. It's what happens when you take the open-source ethos — free as in freedom, not just free as in beer — and apply it to physical space.

§ 05.2The Smile Metric

Everyone looks depressed in airports. This is not a coincidence — it's the output of a system designed for extraction, not habitation. The measure of The Commons is simpler than any KPI: do people smile here? Do strangers talk to each other? Can someone who doesn't speak the dominant language still feel welcome? Can a deaf person and a hearing person learn to communicate because the space invites it?

If the architecture of a space determines the behavior of the people in it, then the architecture of public space is a moral question.

Build spaces that assume you're a person, not a consumer. The rest follows.
